# UW Financial Analysis: Funko, Boeing, and FedEx

[Read the original report](./UW-Financial-Analysis.pdf)

## Project summary

I worked with a team on a financial analysis report for B BUS 350 - Business Finance. The report examines Funko, Boeing, and FedEx, using financial statements, competitor comparisons, historical trends, and earnings-call discussions to explain each company's performance and risks.

My focus in presenting this project is straightforward: a useful analysis needs to explain what a ratio means for the business. Revenue growth, profitability, cash generation, and debt can tell different stories. Looking at them together gives a stronger basis for asking questions.

## My contribution

I completed the Funko analysis and contributed to the report summaries. For Funko, I examined the financial results, compared the company with Hasbro and Mattel, and evaluated management's explanations around debt, cash flow, and tariffs.

I wanted my analysis to go beyond reporting a ratio. I explained what the results meant for Funko's financial position and where management's answers left questions unresolved. The Boeing and FedEx sections are included as part of the team's full report.

## What the report covers

### Funko

The Funko section examines changes in revenue, operating income, cash flow, and debt. It compares profitability, liquidity, and leverage with Hasbro and Mattel, and reviews historical net profit margins.

The earnings-call discussion questions whether management's debt-repayment outlook is supported by operating cash flow. It also asks for a clearer explanation of how tariffs affect margins, pricing, and costs.

### Boeing

The Boeing section reviews the report's financial summary and compares the company with Airbus and Lockheed Martin. It examines profitability, liquidity, financing risk, and changes in margins over time.

The earnings-call review connects production targets with supply-chain capacity, workforce investment, safety, and delivery commitments. It highlights the need to understand what must happen operationally for a financial recovery to hold.

### FedEx

The FedEx section compares operating margin, current ratio, and debt-to-equity with UPS and J.B. Hunt. It also reviews historical operating margins and considers whether the DRIVE cost-reduction program is translating into sustained operating improvement.

The earnings-call discussion covers operating leverage, volume recovery, transformation savings, and the proposed separation of FedEx Freight.

## Methods demonstrated

- Financial statement interpretation across the income statement, balance sheet, and cash flow statement.
- Year-over-year comparisons of revenue, earnings, cash flow, and other financial measures.
- Profitability, liquidity, and leverage analysis.
- Peer and industry benchmarking, with attention to comparison categories.
- Historical margin analysis.
- Critical review of management commentary and earnings-call answers.
- Written presentation of financial findings and supporting sources.

## My takeaway

I want to understand whether the business can support the story management is telling. A recovery claim needs evidence in margins and cash flow. A debt-repayment plan needs a credible source of cash. A production target needs the capacity to deliver it.

This project gave me a way to connect those questions to financial statements and explain where the evidence is strong and where more detail is needed.

## Notes on the original submission

The PDF is preserved as submitted. A review identified internal inconsistencies that should be reconciled before relying on its numerical conclusions:

- On page 3, the profitability discussion says all three firms have positive 7% net margins, while the table shows negative margins for Funko and Hasbro. Page 4 recognizes those negative margins.
- The Boeing section gives conflicting descriptions of Airbus leverage and Boeing's equity position. The debt definition and ratio inputs need reconciliation with the cited statements.
- The FedEx operating-margin benchmark is 7.6% in the main comparison table, but one discussion refers to 7.2% as the industry benchmark.
- On page 35, the displayed whole-share quantities multiplied by their stated purchase prices do not equal the listed $3,333.33 holding values. The portfolio needs one consistent convention for whole or fractional shares and any residual cash.

This guide describes the analytical work without treating those unresolved figures as verified investment conclusions.
